## A tax on each unit

A **per-unit tax** is a fixed amount charged on each unit sold, such as ₹6 per litre. Say the seller hands it to the government.

Sellers now need ₹6 more to supply the same amount, so the supply curve moves up by ₹6. The new equilibrium has a higher price for buyers, a lower price kept by sellers, and fewer units traded.

**Tax incidence** means who actually bears the tax. It does not depend on who writes the cheque. It depends on which side can more easily walk away.

## The wedge and deadweight loss

![A ₹10 tax: buyers pay ₹25, sellers keep ₹15, and the red triangle is the deadweight loss](/diagrams/economics/tax-wedge.png)

The grey rectangle is **tax revenue**: tax per unit times units sold. The red triangle is **deadweight loss**: trades that were worth more to the buyer than they cost the seller, but no longer happen because the tax made them unprofitable. Nobody gets that value. It is simply lost.

## Price ceilings and floors

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price. If it is set below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer, so there is a lasting shortage. Rent control and capped fuel prices often lead to queues, waiting lists or black markets.

A **price floor** is a legal minimum. Set above equilibrium, it creates a lasting surplus. Minimum support prices for crops can lead to large unsold government stocks.
