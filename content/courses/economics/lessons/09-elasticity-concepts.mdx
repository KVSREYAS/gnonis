## The definition

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how much the quantity demanded changes when the price changes:

elasticity = (% change in quantity) ÷ (% change in price)

It is negative because price and quantity move in opposite directions, so we usually drop the sign.

- **Elastic** (above 1): a 10% price rise cuts quantity by more than 10%.
- **Inelastic** (below 1): a 10% rise cuts quantity by less than 10%.
- **Unit elastic** (exactly 1): the two percentages match.

## Midpoint method

Percent changes depend on the starting point: ₹10 to ₹12 is +20%, but ₹12 to ₹10 is −17%. The **midpoint method** divides by the average of the two values so the answer is the same either way:

% change in Q = (Q₁ − Q₀) ÷ ((Q₀ + Q₁)/2)

Example: price ₹10 → ₹12, quantity 100 → 80.
% ΔQ = −20 ÷ 90 = −22.2%. % ΔP = 2 ÷ 11 = 18.2%. Elasticity = 22.2 ÷ 18.2 ≈ 1.22, so elastic.

## What makes demand elastic

Demand is more elastic when:

1. **Close substitutes exist.** One brand of biscuits is elastic; food as a whole is not.
2. **It is a large share of your budget.** A 10% rise in rent hurts; a 10% rise in salt goes unnoticed.
3. **It is a want, not a need.** Holidays are more elastic than insulin.
4. **Buyers have time.** Over years people can buy a fuel-efficient car; tomorrow they cannot.

## Elasticity and revenue

Revenue = price × quantity. When the price rises, the first term goes up and the second goes down. Which wins?

- **Inelastic demand**: quantity falls less than price rises, so revenue **rises**.
- **Elastic demand**: quantity falls more, so revenue **falls**.
- **Unit elastic**: revenue stays the same.

This is why governments tax cigarettes and fuel. Demand is inelastic, so the tax raises a lot of money without cutting sales much.
