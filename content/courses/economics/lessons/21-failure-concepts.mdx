## Externalities

An **externality** is a cost or benefit of an activity that falls on people who are not buying or selling it.

- **Negative**: a factory's smoke harms neighbours. The factory does not pay for that harm, so it makes more than is good for society.
- **Positive**: your vaccination protects people around you. You do not get paid for that, so too few people vaccinate.

Fixes: a tax equal to the harm (a **Pigouvian tax**), subsidies for positive externalities, rules, or tradable permits.

## Rival and excludable

Two questions sort goods:

- **Rival**: does one person's use reduce what is left for others? (A sandwich is rival. A radio broadcast is not.)
- **Excludable**: can people who do not pay be stopped from using it? (A cinema is excludable. Street lighting is not.)

| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rival** | Private goods | Common resources |
| **Non-rival** | Club goods | Public goods |

## Public goods and the commons

A **public good** (national defence, flood barriers, basic research) is non-rival and non-excludable. Everyone can benefit without paying, so people **free ride** and private markets make too little. Governments usually fund them through taxes.

A **common resource** (fish in the sea, groundwater, clean air) is rival but non-excludable. Each user gains the full benefit of taking more but shares the cost of depletion with everyone. The result, named by Garrett Hardin in 1968, is the **tragedy of the commons**: overuse until the resource collapses.

## Asymmetric information

**Asymmetric information** means one side of a deal knows something important the other does not.

- **Adverse selection**: hidden information before the deal. Owners of bad used cars are the most eager to sell. George Akerlof showed in 1970 that this can drive good cars out of the market.
- **Moral hazard**: hidden actions after the deal. Once insured, people may take less care.

Fixes include inspections, warranties, reputations and disclosure rules.
