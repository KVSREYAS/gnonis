## GDP

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a period, usually a quarter or a year. "Final" means things sold to the end user, so the flour inside a loaf is not counted twice.

**Real GDP** removes the effect of price changes. Its growth rate is the usual measure of how fast an economy grows. A **recession** is a period when real GDP falls, often defined as two quarters in a row.

## Unemployment

The **labour force** is people working plus people without work who are actively looking. The **unemployment rate** is the share of the labour force without work.

Some unemployment always exists as people change jobs. The rate when the economy is neither overheating nor slack is the **natural rate**. When the economy runs hot, unemployment falls below it and wages and prices rise faster. In this course's simulator the natural rate is 5%.

## The policy rate

A **central bank** (the RBI in India, the Fed in the US) manages money and credit. Its main tool is the **policy rate**: the rate at which it lends to banks (in India, the **repo rate**).

![Policy rate up, loans dearer, spending down, demand and hiring slow, prices rise more slowly](/diagrams/economics/rate-transmission.png)

Raising the rate makes loans dearer across the economy, so households and firms spend less. That cools demand and inflation, at the cost of slower growth and fewer jobs for a while.

## Expectations and the trade-off

In the short run, pushing demand up lowers unemployment and raises inflation. This trade-off is called the **Phillips curve**.

But if inflation stays high, people start to **expect** it and build it into wages and prices. Then the same unemployment comes with ever higher inflation. In the long run there is no trade-off: unemployment returns to its natural rate, and only inflation is left higher.

So central banks try to keep inflation expectations **anchored** near the target.
