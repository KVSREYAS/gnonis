## Dominance

Strategy A **strictly dominates** strategy B if A gives you a higher payoff than B **whatever** the other players do. Then B is **strictly dominated**.

If one strategy dominates all your others, it is a **dominant strategy**: you should play it without even thinking about the other player.

Check it cell by cell: compare your payoff in row A with row B in every column. If A wins in every column, A dominates.

## The prisoner's dilemma

Two suspects are questioned apart. Each can stay **quiet** (cooperate with the other) or **confess** (defect).

![Confess is the better reply to both of the other's choices](/diagrams/game-theory/prisoners-dilemma.png)

If they stay quiet, confessing gets you 0 instead of −1. If they confess, confessing gets you −5 instead of −10. Confess dominates. Both reason this way, both confess, and both get −5, though both staying quiet gives −1 each. Individually rational choices lead to a result that is worse for both.

## Where it shows up

The same structure appears whenever each side gains from cheating but everyone loses if all cheat:

- Two firms that could both keep prices high, but each gains by undercutting.
- Countries that would all gain from cutting emissions, but each gains by letting others do it.
- Teammates who each gain by slacking on a group project.

The usual payoffs used in this course: both cooperate 3 each, both defect 1 each, defect against a cooperator 5 for the defector and 0 for the other.

## Iterated elimination

Some games have no dominant strategy, but still solve. **Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies** works like this:

1. Delete any strictly dominated strategy for any player.
2. The game is now smaller. Look again: a strategy that was fine before may now be dominated.
3. Repeat until nothing more can be deleted.

If one cell is left, that is the prediction. Each round of deletion assumes one more level of "I know that you know that I am rational".
