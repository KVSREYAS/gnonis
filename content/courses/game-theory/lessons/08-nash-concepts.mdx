## Best responses

Your **best response** to the other player's choice is the strategy that gives you the highest payoff against it.

A quick method for a matrix:

1. For each column, mark your highest payoff (gold) in that column.
2. For each row, mark their highest payoff (green) in that row.
3. Any cell with **both** numbers marked is a Nash equilibrium.

In the prisoner's dilemma only Confess/Confess gets both marks.

## Nash equilibrium

A **Nash equilibrium** is a strategy profile where no player can get a higher payoff by changing **only their own** strategy. Everyone is already playing a best response to everyone else.

It is named after John Nash, who proved in 1950 that every finite game has at least one, if players are allowed to randomise (module 4).

An equilibrium need not be good for the players. Both confessing is an equilibrium and is worse for both than both staying quiet.

## Several equilibria: coordination

In the **stag hunt**, two hunters can chase a stag (only works if both do) or a hare (works alone).

![Stag hunt: Stag/Stag and Hare/Hare are both equilibria](/diagrams/game-theory/stag-hunt.png)

Both Stag/Stag (4, 4) and Hare/Hare (3, 3) are equilibria. Stag is better for both but risky: if the other goes for hare, you get 0. Games like this are **coordination games**. The theory says which outcomes are stable, but not which one people will reach.

## Focal points and no pure equilibrium

Thomas Schelling noticed in 1960 that people often coordinate without talking by picking what stands out: a **focal point**. Asked to meet a stranger in New York with no plan, most of his students chose Grand Central Station at noon.

Some games have no cell where both are happy. In **matching pennies**, you win if the coins match and they win if not.

![Matching pennies: no cell is a best response for both](/diagrams/game-theory/matching-pennies.png)

The answer there is to randomise, which is module 4.
