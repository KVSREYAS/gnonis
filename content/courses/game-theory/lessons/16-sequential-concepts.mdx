## Backward induction

In a **sequential game** players move one after another, and each sees what was done before.

To solve one, use **backward induction**:

1. Go to every last decision in the tree. Pick the move that gives the mover the highest payoff.
2. Replace that decision with the payoffs it leads to.
3. Step back one level and repeat, until you reach the first move.

The result is called a **subgame perfect equilibrium**: every player makes a best move at every point where they might have to move.

## Credible and empty threats

![Entry game: New enters, Old shares, payoffs (2, 2)](/diagrams/game-theory/entry-tree.png)

An old firm says: "If you enter, I start a price war." Would it? If New has entered, a price war gives Old 1 and sharing gives Old 2. Old would share. So the threat is **not credible**, and New should enter.

A **credible threat** is one the player would really carry out when the time comes. To make a threat credible, firms commit in advance: build extra capacity, sign contracts, or burn bridges.

## First-mover advantage and commitment

Moving first can help when it lets you commit. If you can take an action the other player sees and cannot ignore, you shape their best reply.

But moving first can also hurt, when the second mover learns from your move. In rock-paper-scissors played in turns, going first loses every time.

Backward induction tells you which case you are in.
