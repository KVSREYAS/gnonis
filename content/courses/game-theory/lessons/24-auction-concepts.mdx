## Four formats

- **English**: the price rises in public until one bidder is left. Used for art and cricket player auctions.
- **Dutch**: the price falls until someone accepts. Used for flowers in the Netherlands.
- **First-price sealed bid**: everyone submits one secret bid. Highest bid wins and pays **its own bid**. Common in government tenders.
- **Second-price sealed bid** (Vickrey): highest bid wins but pays the **second-highest bid**. Online ad auctions are close cousins.

In a **private value** auction, each bidder knows what the item is worth to them, and values differ.

## Second price: bid your value

William Vickrey showed in 1961 that in a second-price auction, bidding exactly your value is a **dominant strategy**.

- Bid more than your value: you only change the outcome when someone else bid between your value and your bid. Then you win and pay more than the item is worth to you.
- Bid less: you only change the outcome when the top rival bid is between your bid and your value. Then you lose an item you would have bought at a profit.

Your bid decides whether you win, never what you pay. So tell the truth.

## First price: shade

In a first-price auction you pay your own bid. Bid your value and your profit is zero even when you win. So you **shade**: bid below your value.

How much? With n bidders whose values are spread evenly, the equilibrium bid is (n − 1)/n of your value. With 2 bidders, bid half; with 4, bid three quarters. More rivals, less shading.

## Revenue equivalence and the winner's curse

**Revenue equivalence**: with private values spread the same way and risk-neutral bidders, all four formats give the seller the same expected revenue. Bidders shade in first-price exactly enough to offset the second-price discount.

When the item has a **common value** (an oil field worth the same to everyone, but nobody knows how much), the winner is the one who guessed highest, and often overpaid. That is the **winner's curse**. Smart bidders bid below their estimate to allow for it.
