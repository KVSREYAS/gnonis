## The ultimatum game

Two players split ₹10. The **proposer** offers an amount to the **responder**. If the responder accepts, both get the split. If they reject, both get nothing.

Backward induction: a responder who only cares about money accepts any positive offer, so the proposer offers ₹1 and keeps ₹9.

In experiments since Güth and colleagues (1982), proposers offer about 40% on average, and offers below about 20% are often rejected. People pay to punish what they see as unfair.

## Social preferences

**Social preferences** means caring about outcomes for others, not only yourself: fairness, reciprocity (being kind to the kind and harsh to the harsh), or dislike of inequality.

They do not break game theory. They change the payoffs: if a ₹1 offer feels insulting enough, rejecting it really is your best reply. Good predictions use the payoffs people actually feel.

## Public goods and free riding

In a **public goods game**, each player can put money into a shared pot. The pot is multiplied (say by 2) and split equally among everyone. The group does best if all contribute, but each person does best by keeping their money and taking a share of what others put in: **free riding**.

In experiments, people start by contributing about half, then contributions fall round by round. When players can pay to punish free riders, contributions stay high (Fehr and Gächter, 2000).
