## Two ceilings

The **roofline model** plots attainable performance against arithmetic intensity on log-log axes:

$$P = \min(P_{peak},\; I \times BW)$$

On the left, the sloped roof: you are **memory-bound** and speed equals bandwidth times
intensity. On the right, the flat roof: you are **compute-bound** at peak FLOP/s.

## The ridge point

Where the two roofs meet, $I^* = P_{peak} / BW$. Kernels left of it waste compute, and kernels
right of it waste bandwidth.

| GPU | Peak dense BF16 / FP16 | HBM bandwidth | Ridge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L4 | 121 TFLOP/s | 0.3 TB/s | ~400 |
| A100 80GB | 312 TFLOP/s | 2.0 TB/s | ~156 |
| H100 SXM | 989 TFLOP/s | 3.35 TB/s | ~295 |

## Reading a roofline

A roofline plot has arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte) on the x-axis and achievable FLOP/s on the y-axis. It has two roofs:

- A **sloped roof** on the left, set by memory bandwidth: the more maths per byte, the higher you can go.
- A **flat roof** on the right, set by peak FLOP/s.

They meet at the **ridge point**. Left of it, a kernel is memory-bound: more bandwidth or more reuse per byte helps, extra FLOP/s don't. Right of it, a kernel is compute-bound: only more FLOP/s, or cheaper maths such as 8-bit matmuls, helps.

Two ways to move a kernel right: reuse each loaded byte more (bigger batch, bigger tiles), or store values in fewer bits (fp8 or int4 weights), which raises FLOPs per byte without changing the maths.
