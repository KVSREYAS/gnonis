## Outlier channels

Dettmers et al. (LLM.int8()) showed that above about 6.7B parameters, a handful of hidden dimensions carry huge activation values. Naive int8 activation quantization destroys accuracy. Their fix keeps those channels in fp16, which is accurate but slower.

## Smooth it over

SmoothQuant divides each activation channel by s_j and multiplies the matching weight row by s_j. α sets how much range moves: near 0 the activations keep it, near 1 the weights take nearly all of it, and 0.5 splits it evenly. Both sides then quantize well to int8, so the layer can run as **W8A8** (8-bit weights and 8-bit activations, with an int8 matmul).

Why not just give each activation channel its own scale? In Y = XW, channel j of X is the inner dimension of the matmul, the one that is summed over. A fast int8 matmul can only apply scales per row of X (per token) or per column of W (per output) after the sum. A per-channel scale on X would be needed inside the sum. SmoothQuant moves that per-channel factor into W, where it can be applied once, offline.

Sources: [LLM.int8()](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339), [SmoothQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438)

## Why the maths is unchanged

For one output, Y = Σⱼ Xⱼ Wⱼ, summing over channels j. SmoothQuant replaces Xⱼ with Xⱼ / sⱼ and Wⱼ with sⱼ · Wⱼ. Each term becomes (Xⱼ / sⱼ)(sⱼ · Wⱼ) = Xⱼ Wⱼ, so the sum is identical.

In practice the division is folded into the previous layer's weights (for example the LayerNorm scale), so it costs nothing at run time.

Before rounding, outputs match up to floating-point rounding, typically tiny relative differences in fp16. The accuracy effect only appears when both tensors are rounded to int8, and that is where smoothing helps: the activations no longer have outliers that stretch their scale.
