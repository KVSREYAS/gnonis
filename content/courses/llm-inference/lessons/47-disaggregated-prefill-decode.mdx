## Interference

In one engine, a long prefill delays every decode step (worse TPOT), and decodes eat into prefill time (worse TTFT). Chunked prefill softens this. Disaggregation removes it.

## Two GPU pools

The prefill pool runs the prompt, produces the first token and the KV cache, and ships the KV over NVLink or RDMA (remote direct memory access, a GPU-to-GPU copy across machines that skips the CPU) to the decode pool. The decode pool continues from the second token. Each pool picks its own GPU count and parallelism: prefill GPUs can use small batches and more tensor parallelism for fast TTFT, decode GPUs large batches for throughput.

DistServe (OSDI 2024) places the two pools to maximise goodput. Splitwise (Microsoft) runs prefill and decode on different machine types, even different GPU generations. Mooncake (Moonshot AI) builds the design around a large KV cache pool kept in CPU memory and SSD across the cluster.

Sources: [DistServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670), [Splitwise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18677), [Mooncake](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079)

## What DistServe measured

DistServe (OSDI 2024) defines **goodput** as the highest request rate a system can serve while meeting its latency targets (TTFT and TPOT) for a chosen share of requests, measured per GPU.

It reported serving up to 7.4x more requests, or targets 12.6x tighter, than state-of-the-art systems, while keeping over 90% of requests within their targets.

The KV transfer is smaller than it sounds. A 512-token OPT-66B request has about 1.13 GB of KV cache. On a cluster whose nodes were linked by only 25 Gbps, DistServe placed the prefill and decode stages so that transfers stayed on NVLink inside a node, and transfer took under 0.1% of total latency.

Source: [DistServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)
