## Sweep the load

Fire requests at increasing rates (Poisson arrivals) and record per-request TTFT and TPOT. Plot p50 and p99 against the rate. While the arrival rate is below what the server can process, queues stay short and latency is flat. Once arrivals exceed that rate, the queue grows every second and TTFT climbs steeply. The rate where the curve bends upward (the **knee**) is your capacity.

![p50 and p99 latency against request rate, flat then shooting up at the saturation knee](/diagrams/llm-inference/12-capstone/03-benchmark-and-profile.png)

## Why tails matter

Users notice the slow requests, and they send many requests. If 1% of requests are slow, a user who sends 20 a day has a 1 − 0.99²⁰ ≈ 18% chance of meeting at least one slow request every day.

That is why latency targets are set on percentiles such as p95 or p99, not on the mean. A slow tail usually has a specific cause that the mean hides: long prompts stalling decode, bursts filling the queue, or preemption repeating work.

When benchmarking, use random (Poisson) arrivals rather than evenly spaced ones, because bursts create the queues that cause tail latency.

## Check the roofline

**Model bandwidth utilisation (MBU)** = achieved bytes per second / peak HBM bandwidth, where achieved bytes per second = tokens/s × bytes read per token (weights, plus KV at long context). It is the decode counterpart of MFU from module 4. At batch 1, an MBU far below 60% means overhead: Python, kernel launches, sampling.

Sources: [Databricks: LLM inference performance (MBU)](https://www.databricks.com/blog/llm-inference-performance-engineering-best-practices), [vLLM benchmarks](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/tree/main/benchmarks)
